# Simplification by photo type

Read only the applicable section or sections.

## Portrait or visible face

- Preserve head angle, gaze, hairstyle silhouette, expression, and one or two recognition cues.
- Crop distracting shoulders or background when they do not support the portrait.
- Reduce the face to the approved sparse mark set; never compensate for likeness by adding realistic facial detail.

## Outfit or full-body photo

- Preserve overall body line, stance, weight shift, hand position, clothing silhouette, hem lengths, and distinctive color blocking.
- Simplify fabric folds to a few structural lines.
- Omit minor seams, stitching, logos, jewelry, and shoe construction unless identity depends on them.
- Keep long limbs readable but allow slight naive proportion; do not turn the body into fashion-rendering realism or chibi anatomy.

## Person and pet

- Preserve contact, gaze, distance, and relative scale before individual detail.
- Keep recognizable pet silhouette, ears, tail, major markings, and posture.
- Simplify fur into contour rhythm and a few masses; no strand-by-strand fur.

## Indoor daily life

- Keep the furniture or surface that explains the action: bed, sofa, chair, table, counter, or window.
- Reduce the room to one to three anchors. Remove décor, clutter, wall texture, cables, labels, and repeated objects.
- Maintain overlaps that explain sitting, leaning, holding, reading, eating, or resting.

## Outdoor or travel

- Keep only the landmark cue that establishes place or activity: horizon, rock contour, road line, mountain edge, railing, wind turbine, boat, or sign silhouette.
- Remove atmospheric detail, foliage texture, distant crowds, and photographic depth.
- Use negative space instead of drawing sky, sea, or terrain as filled scenery unless a restrained color field is essential.

## Still life, food, or everyday object

- Preserve the primary silhouette, opening, handle, label block, overlap, and use relationship.
- Replace material realism with one or two internal cues.
- Remove reflections, texture, tiny printed text, packaging noise, and secondary objects.
- Use isolated composition and generous space; add small motion or emphasis marks only when they clarify use.

## Multiple subjects

- Identify the main relationship and establish a clear hierarchy.
- Preserve relative positions, facing direction, and contact points.
- Simplify secondary subjects more aggressively than the primary subject.
- Do not make every face equally detailed.
